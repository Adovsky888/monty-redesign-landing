AI Shorts Generator: Three Kinds and Which One You Actually Need

Shorts generators come in three kinds: text-to-video, clipping long recordings, and building a reel from one take. Strengths of each and how to pick for your case.

Guide · 2026-08-24 · 6 min read

Max GPTBusiness development at Monty

Three different machines are sold under the label AI shorts generator. The first turns text into video: stock footage, synthetic voiceover, sometimes an avatar. The second clips highlights out of long recordings: podcasts, streams, webinars. The third builds a finished reel from one short phone take. All three honestly qualify as shorts generators, and they barely compete with each other, because they start from different source material. Picking the wrong kind costs more than picking the wrong service within a kind.

Kind 1: text to video, faceless channels

Services like InVideo or Zebracat take a text prompt and assemble a video from stock, captions and a synthetic voice. This is the engine of faceless channels: mass production, zero filming, topics from motivation to top-5 facts. The strength is volume: dozens of videos a day without a camera. The weakness is trust: viewers see no person, they subscribe to a format rather than an author, and monetizing that audience with expertise is hard.

Kind 2: clipping long recordings

OpusClip, Munch and similar services solve the podcaster's problem: there is an hour of conversation, the AI finds the strong moments and returns a stack of vertical clips with captions. A great fit when long-form content already ships regularly. The limit is baked into the source: with no hour-long recording there is nothing to clip, and a clip stays a fragment of someone's longer context instead of a thought built for the format.

Kind 3: a reel from one take

The third kind starts from a short piece of filming rather than text or an hour of tape. That is how Monty works: you record one take on your phone; the script gets written in your voice, pauses and false starts get cut, captions sync to your speech, music and b-roll drop in, per-platform versions get built, and the video goes out on its own to YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, TikTok and Telegram. You are on camera, the entry cost is five minutes, and no long source is needed.

A quick test: what do you have as input? Only ideas - text to video. An archive of long streams - clipping. The will to film five phone minutes every couple of days - building from a take.

Captions and cadence decide in every kind

Whichever kind you pick, two factors work the same way. First, captions: in the Verizon Media and Publicis Media study, 80% of viewers are more likely to finish a captioned video. Second, cadence: Buffer's look at 11.4 million posts found roughly +17% views per post at 2-5 posts a week. So the winning pipeline is the one you can actually feed every week, never the one with the prettiest demo. Monty gives you three videos a month free: testing on your own take once beats a month of reading reviews.

FAQ

Which AI shorts generator is the best?

Depends on your source material. For a faceless channel - text-to-video. For a podcaster with an archive - a clipper like OpusClip. For an expert with five spare minutes - building from a take, like Monty. Services within a kind are close; the gap between kinds is huge.

Can I make shorts with no filming at all?

Yes, text-to-video generators assemble a video from stock and a synthetic voice. It works for faceless formats, but a personal brand does not grow that way: audiences follow a live person on camera.

How is building from a take different from clipping a long video?

Clipping searches an existing hour of tape for moments. Building from a take constructs the reel from scratch for the format: a script in your voice, editing, captions and publishing out of five minutes of filming.

Does Monty publish shorts by itself?

Yes: YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, TikTok and Telegram, each platform getting its own version. Keep approval manual or switch on scheduled autopilot.
