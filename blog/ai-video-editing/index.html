AI Video Editing: Turn One Take Into a Posted Reel

How AI video editing handles the whole edit for you: script, cuts, captions, per-platform versions and posting. See how much time AI saves over editing by hand.

Guide · 2026-07-07 · 9 min read

Alexey ShmidtProduct and marketing at Monty

You shoot a decent take in five minutes, then spend three hours trimming cuts, fixing captions word by word and exporting a separate version for every platform. The shoot is quick, the edit is endless, and the edit is what kills the urge to shoot again. AI video editing flips that ratio: you hand over the footage and get back a finished video that is already laid out across your networks.

Below we break down, step by step, what the AI actually takes on, how much time it frees up, and how the approach differs from editing by hand in CapCut or Premiere. With numbers from real studies, not vibes.

What actually eats the time

Editing a short vertical clip by hand is not a ten-minute job. Editing studios put a clip under two minutes at one to six hours of work, and a one-minute video with proper styling at anywhere from half an hour for a simple cut to three or four hours for a complex one (Offshore Clipping). And that is one version, before re-uploading to four platforms.

The manual timeline chokes the whole pipeline at once. While you proof captions and hunt for music on one clip, the feed sits still. That is why short video stalls at the edit rather than the shoot. Filming is quick; assembling and posting is what eats the day.

Count the context-switching, not just the hours. Every clip is a chain of shoot, edit in one app, caption in another, upload in five places. An AI editor collapses that chain into a single pass.

Why nobody watches a clip without captions

Short video gets watched on mute. According to publisher reports, up to 85% of feed video views happen with the sound off (Digiday). With no captions, the viewer catches nothing and scrolls on. So text on screen is not decoration; it is the condition for the clip getting watched at all.

It works the other way too. A survey of 5,616 people by Verizon Media and Publicis Media found that 80% of viewers are more likely to finish a video when captions are present (3Play Media). Platform measurements back it up: captions lift average watch time by roughly 12%, and for some brands by as much as 25% (3Play Media).

No surprise captions became the default. Across 13.5 million short clips, OpusClip found captions on 80.2% of them, almost all animated and timed to speech. It is the single most adopted editing feature in short-form video (OpusClip). Typing that text by hand, word by word, is slow, which is exactly why AI has taken the job over.

What the AI does from idea to posted video

A strong AI editor covers the whole chain, from a blank screen to a posted video, not just one step:

  • Writes the script in your voice and opens on a hook, not on 'hey guys, so today we're gonna'.
  • Reviews the footage itself: pauses, filler and blown takes gone.
  • Animates the picture: the frame drifts inside a shot and the size changes at every cut.
  • Times captions to your speech and styles them to read on a phone in the feed.
  • Picks music and b-roll to the rhythm, not the first track it finds.
  • Builds a separate version per platform: format, length, text position.
  • Posts it to YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, TikTok and Telegram itself.

Your one step is filming a take to the ready script. From there, two modes: leave approval on and every video waits for your go, or move it all to a schedule and never open it by hand. Short video under 60 seconds, best in the 31-60 second range, has ranked as the highest-ROI format for marketers several years running (HubSpot), so a steady stream of these clips is precisely the thing worth automating.

The camera never sits still: the shot size changes on its own

The least visible and most felt part of an edit is movement. A phone take is one static camera: you stand in frame and talk, and the framing never changes once. Even with perfect captions that reads like a recorded call. Monty animates the picture by default: inside a shot the frame drifts slowly (the Ken Burns move), and at every cut the shot size changes - closer, wider, closer again.

  • Shot size is derived from your footage. The engine measures how much of the frame your head takes and picks a wide/close pair for that specific take, instead of stretching one template over everyone.
  • The size change lands exactly on the cut, so the join between takes reads as a deliberate edit, not a glitch.
  • The frame is held on your eye line: when the size changes, your head stays on the same line and only the scale jumps.
  • Movement inside a shot is one-way and at a steady speed. A zoom in-and-out pulse reads as breathing on a face, so the engine deliberately never does it.
  • Your head sits in the upper part of the frame and never slides under the caption block.
  • On the key phrase a punch-in kicks in: a short push exactly on the words the video was made for.
Strip out the b-roll, the transitions and the graphics and the base is still there: speech with no dead air, captions on beat and a camera that moves. That alone is enough to get the clip watched through. Everything else sits on top.

Editing by hand versus AI

In CapCut or Premiere you sit at the timeline yourself: you cut, you proof captions, you upload to four platforms. An editor speeds up your work, but the work is still yours. An AI editor takes the work itself. You brief it and check the result, like a human editor without the deadlines and the back-and-forth.

This does not mean throwing CapCut away. Hand the rough edit, captions and posting to the AI, and when one clip needs polishing frame by frame, finish it by hand. You touch only what actually needs hands.

FAQ

Do I need editing skills to use an AI editor?

No. If you can hit record and type a couple of lines, you can run a whole channel. Editing, captions, per-platform versions and posting are all on the AI.

Does the AI really edit the whole video?

Yes. Cuts, music, b-roll, on-beat captions and per-platform versions are assembled automatically from one take. Your part is to shoot the take and approve the result.

How much time does it actually save?

Editing a short clip by hand runs from half an hour to several hours depending on complexity. The AI assembles it in minutes and posts to every network itself, so you also get back the re-upload time.

Do I have to film, or can it cut from an existing video?

Either. The AI can build a clip from a fresh take against a script, or cut short clips out of a long recording such as a webinar, stream or podcast.

If I drop the b-roll and the graphics, is it just a talking head?

No. The base dynamics don't depend on b-roll: speech with no dead air, on-beat captions and a live camera - frame movement inside a shot plus a size change at every cut - are in every video. B-roll, transitions and graphics sit on top of that.
